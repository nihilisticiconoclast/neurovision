    How it reads a digit

    1. Shrink. Your strokes are scaled into a 20 × 20 box and centred by their centre of mass on a 28 × 28 grid, the way the MNIST training digits were prepared. That gives 784 numbers from 0 (blank) to 1 (ink).
    2. Layer 1: edges. Eight filters of 5 × 5 weights slide across the grid. At every spot a filter weighs the 25 pixels beneath it, adds a bias and keeps the total only if it is positive: max(0, w·x + b). Each filter paints a map of where its pattern sits; every 2 × 2 block then keeps its strongest value, shrinking the maps to 14 × 14.
    3. Layer 2: shapes. Sixteen filters slide across all eight maps at once, with 5 × 5 × 8 = 200 weights each, combining edges into curves, corners, stroke ends and even the gaps between strokes. Pooling shrinks these maps to 7 × 7.
    4. Output. That leaves 16 × 7 × 7 = 784 numbers, the same count as the pixels, but each now says “this shape, here” rather than “ink, here”. Every digit’s score weighs all 784, and softmax turns the ten scores into probabilities that add up to 100%.

    Trained from scratch in NumPy on 55,000 MNIST digits, with the same mix as the dense Neural Digit Reader: half of every training pass used digits re-drawn as pen strokes with random slant, wobble and pen size, plus flagged European 1s, so mouse and finger drawings look familiar. __REDRAW_ACC__ correct on the test digits re-drawn that way. Because each filter is reused at every position, the whole network needs __PARAMS_NOTE__. It runs entirely in your browser.
